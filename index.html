Dragonfruit OS

Ideation Engine — Workflow

What

One portal where a Creative Director connects a client's YouTube channel and knowledge base. The engine pulls a wide but curated data set — from on and off of YouTube, but weighted to overindex on YouTube-native signals — refines it against the client's persona and DFM's standards, and presents topics the CD iterates on live: a gamified loop that only ends when every standard is hit. Each locked topic ships with a research brief, sample packaging, and a client-calibrated predictive score, and the system self-scores against the YouTube Performance Standard's locked 7-day target. The goal isn't faster CDs — it's breaking the quality↔quantity tradeoff by 10×-ing research breadth and depth so synthesis happens naturally.

How — the architecture, in three layers

1 · Data (the Dragonfruit Data Lake). Raw records at rest: Turso performance snapshots, the prediction ledger, A/B split-test results, and each client's knowledge base. On its own this is just data — it answers nothing.

2 · Processing (the agents). This is where the intelligence lives. The research agent turns queries into compiled research; the scoring agent runs the prediction model against the lake; the tracking agent writes snapshots in; the recalibration agent refits the standards monthly. Agents are what turn stored data into answers — the lake without them is a filing cabinet.

3 · Craft (the CD Brain). Hand-fed DFM historical craft knowledge — Adrian's Looms, Content Strategy Grains, and key CD SOPs. It is not a box on this map or a thing you open: skills reach into it behind the scenes, so it is already underneath every answer the engine gives.

When a CD connects a client, the session is scoped to that client's knowledge base and calibration. The data lake stays shared and read-only during ideation — every session benchmarks against the same cross-client pool — and writes back are keyed by client, so ten CDs running ten clients simultaneously never collide.

The two-layer principle

The process layer — engine, agents, skills, standards — is the shared product. The personal layer — each client's knowledge base, calibration, and lessons — is sealed per client: never shared across sessions and never packaged into the product, enforced in tooling rather than policy. The engine never invents a client fact or the client's voice; client truth comes only from the knowledge base, so the CD's time goes only to taste and judgment.

Gamified CD portal — design notes

  • Deal (Pull). Each pull deals the CD a hand of topic cards — potential titles + loglines of episodes — each showing its sources and an early score. Weak cards are discarded; standard-qualifying ones that aren't picked go to the Vault.
  • Review (Validate). The CD sweeps the hand against the three checks — YouTube demand (real, organic, durable), an off-YouTube signal, and Topic Craft Standards — killing or re-angling what fails.
  • Refine (Shape). Opening a card shows the standards board: every client-fit and craft standard as a live meter that re-scores as the CD edits angle, title, and thumbnail. Change one word, watch the meter move. No lock until every meter is green.
  • Lock (Benchmark & greenlight). The scoring agent delivers the predictive score against Turso and the locked target. Clears the bar → the topic locks to the slate, ready to share with the client. Misses → back to Pull with the learnings.
  • The meta-game. Track each CD's calling average — how often their locked topics actually went green at 7 days. Prediction accuracy becomes the CD's score too, not just the model's.
  • Deliberately excluded: leaderboards ranking CDs against each other. Channels differ wildly in difficulty and the multiple absorbs that difference, so the game is always CD-vs-the-bar, never CD-vs-CD.

Engine design rules

  • No comment mining. Comments are under 1% of viewers and a self-selecting group (superfans and haters) — noise, not demand. Engagement rate is an acceptable signal; comment content is not.
  • Expertise-fit is a hard filter. Demand only counts if the client can genuinely deliver on it. Chasing demand the client can't credibly serve produces faked videos that fail — validate against what they're truly expert in.
  • Demand over supply. Don't just clone viral videos. Use outliers to extrapolate the audience's pain points and aspirations, then ideate from those problems — including combining two demand signals into one original concept.
  • Validate the theme, not the video. A single 40× upload is an anecdote; a vein — many videos with sustained views-per-video — is demand evidence. Boolean the theme before greenlighting anything.
  • Comparison-set hygiene. Long-form only, Shorts and boosted excluded, before any multiple is computed. A baseline inflated by Shorts measures the wrong thing.
  • The transcript is the only source of words. Scripts shape the client's interview words — never invent. Hook and close are the only exceptions.
  • The recency rule. A client's new topic suggestion (Slack or the living survey) must surface in the very next ideation round — or get an explicit CD response explaining why not. Silence reads as being ignored.
